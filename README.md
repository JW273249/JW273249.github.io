# Reflection Preamble

On the first day of the Master's course, we were questioned on why we are here. Not as an attack on our personal journey, but to truly consider the value of this Post Graduate studies would provide us. There is of course the extra year before getting a 'real' job or truly stepping into the disaster that is the 'Grown-up world.' But I have always wanted to do my Masters. I know what I want. I know who I am.

I am a Team Leader, I enjoy being the person people go to, and absolutely love being the person who people can rely on to make them the best version of themselves they can. Each year in my Undergrad, I became the Scrum Master/Agile Leader. Whilst overcoming the new systems and formats that are embedded into this style of project management (as well as learning how to make games, a completely new venture for myself) I found myself truly enjoying the semblance of responsibility and inter-personal relationships with my teams. I knew that above all else, the skill I wanted to develop most here was this leadership.

Then we come onto the Professional practices I have learnt at Falmouth to work in this role: Agile and Scrum. They are undeniably the better suited system for game development. They are not perfect, but fundamentally they are a set of guidance's and rules of thumb for project development. To co-op George Orwell and his writing tips, break any of the 'above tips' before doing something stupid *citation needed*. That is how I have co-opted the Agile framework. Given how fluid a game project can be (with everything changing sometimes within hours of a decision being made) not being too rigid and allowing change has benefited not only the full outcome of a project, but also allowed my team members to grow as well. My ethos of leadership is not to be the most dominant voice, or the final say; I merely try to be the pedestal on which everyone can be the voice to be heard. I am no artist, and my programming is negligible compared to dedicated programmers. But if someone in the team is the best person to speak to, but struggles in that elevated role, then I can be a mouth piece to facilitate their sharing of knowledge and opinion. I want each member in my team to be the best version of them- because if we are all great at what we do, then it will be reflected in the final project.

I also learnt, it is important to have documentation that can grow with a project, but also as something to look back on, clarify and corroborate the next step in production. Typically, at Falmouth we used various forms of Trello/Teams Planner boards. These were easy to quickly scan through to see a checklist of tasks to be done, provide a documented history of the project and see where the future of the project is going. But my use of these has been lackluster, or downright abandoned at times. Despite knowing roughly what was going on, at times even I was often out of the loop, or behind on the evolution of an idea. It was frustrating, and completely avoidable. I therefore began to understand the value of transparency and visibility when people are typically looking at me for support. If my team know what I am doing, what I am thinking, it can help them make an educated decision on what they should also be doing. 

Therefore, for this reflective project, I intend to look into improving my project organisation skills and practices. I will begin by researching the various organisation tools already used by game developers, and cost willing choose something to create my artefact. At first glance, potentially creating a fake game project with a dedicated Miro Board to collate everything for that project. A living GDD. I will also look into practices on how to maintain such a tool, and avoid my previous failings where all documents die and are abandoned. With a priority of expanding my transparency and visibility to a team, and have a tangible space to see how development of a project is going.
